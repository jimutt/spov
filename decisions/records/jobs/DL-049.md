## Context

An unattended job needs a bound, or one stuck in `blocked` on a question nobody answers, or one taking long turns, holds a billable sandbox until the sandbox's own timeout. Parking a job on a bound does not help, because a parked job still holds the sandbox the bound exists to stop. Failing a job at the bound throws away a report that was mostly written.

The bounds other than time are poor measures. A count of turns or continuations measures neither cost nor progress: a job with forty short productive turns is cut off while one with three hour-long turns is not, and turns cannot be counted reliably from the server's side, because a manual compaction is admitted into the run already in flight, so a prompt, a compaction and a second prompt are one execution and a count of `session.execution.succeeded` undercounts. Time is the only budget on useful work.

## Decision

### One allowance for the whole job

A job has one wall-clock allowance, `MaxDuration`, fixed at creation. Time in `running` or `blocked` counts (`JobState.ClockCounts`); `queued`, `provisioning`, `steered` and `delivering` do not.

- `blocked` counts because it is unattended: the job is waiting on a person who may never come, and the sandbox bills throughout.
- Steering pauses the clock. A person who steers is watching, and cutting them off because the job started an hour earlier would penalise the supervision the bound stands in for.
- The row keeps a ledger. `ClockUsed` is the total of finished stretches in whole seconds (`clock_used_seconds`, so the memory and Postgres stores agree), and `ClockStartedAt` is the start of the current stretch, nil while the clock is stopped. `StampJobTimes` maintains it: entering a counting state from a non-counting one sets the start, leaving one banks the stretch into `ClockUsed` and clears the start, and a move between `running` and `blocked` changes neither. The totals are on the row, so restarting a replica loses nothing. Elapsed time is `ClockUsed` plus the current stretch (`Job.ClockElapsed`).
- A resume continues with what is left. No act grants a fresh allowance: attaching, a queued message, steering and resuming do not. A job resumed with little time left runs that little and is then delivered; more time needs a new job (`TestTheWallClockIsOneAllowanceAcrossTheJob`, `TestAResumedJobContinuesItsClock`).
- Delivery is outside the allowance. `WallClockMargin` covers the launch before the clock and the delivery after it.
- `JobView` carries `clock_used`. A client computes when a counting job's clock runs out as `clock_started_at + max_duration - clock_used`, and how much is left when it is paused. `spov jobs show` and the session page say so ("Time limit", with when it runs out or why it is not counting; `wallClock` in `web/src/lib/format.ts`). The verdict's bound note names the total time used.

No count of turns or continuations ends a run. The plugin still counts continuations for its status reply and evidence log, with no limit. What does end a run: the agent declaring completion, a person's stop or cancel, the wall clock, a continuation that cannot be sent (`continue-failed`, which also keeps a loop that cannot send from spinning), a completion tool that could not be registered (`no-completion-tool`), repeated failing turns, and the repeated-denial stop (DL-048, DL-040). The repeated-denial stop is a conduct safeguard, not a budget: it ends a run that keeps trying what its policy forbids and does not cap a run that is making progress, and docs and copy describe it that way.

### Where the duration comes from

The first layer that is set wins:

1. `spov jobs create --max-duration`, or the web form's choice.
2. The CLI config key `sandbox.job_max_duration`, sent with `max_duration_preferred: true`.
3. The profile's `default_job_duration`, set on the settings page (DL-062) through `PATCH /api/v1/me` (`users.default_job_duration_seconds`, 0 is unset).
4. The built-in `jobs.DefaultWallClock`, 1 hour. It is long enough for the report and `pr` jobs run so far.

The duration is capped by the caller's quota `max_job_duration` (4 hours unless configured, overridable per user; see DL-009) and by the room the sandbox cap leaves after `WallClockMargin`. An explicit duration that does not fit is refused with 422 naming the limit (or, for the sandbox cap, the longest that does fit). A preferred duration, the profile default and the built-in default are clamped instead. A preference is not a request about this job, and a config file shared between servers with different limits must not fail every job; a value typed for this job is refused because it was asked for exactly. A clock under a minute (`MinWallClock`) is refused. The profile default is validated on save as a duration of at least `MinWallClock`, but not against the user's limit, because the limit can change; clamping at create covers that. The job form's default option names its source: "Your default (2h)" when the profile sets one, "1 hour (the default)" otherwise, or "Your limit (…)" when the limit is lower than either (`defaultClockLabel`).

This mirrors how git access resolves for a job (DL-043), so a user learns one rule. `jobClock` resolves it at create (`TestAJobsWallClockIsResolvedAtCreate`).

### The sandbox timeout follows from the clock

A job's sandbox timeout is `max_duration + WallClockMargin + SteerHeadroom`, cut to the caller's `max_timeout` quota and to the sandbox hard cap, `sandbox.MaxTimeout` (12 hours). `WallClockMargin` is the launch budget plus five minutes. `SteerHeadroom` is fixed at one hour. The request's `sandbox.timeout` is cleared before validation for a job so it cannot refuse one, and the config's `sandbox.timeout` applies to interactive sessions only. When the cap is short, the steering room goes first: the clock must fit within the cap minus `WallClockMargin`. One duration describes the job, so a user cannot set a clock the sandbox will not reach and nobody has to work out the margin.

The headroom is an hour because that is enough for a takeover and it bounds a forgotten steered job to about an hour past its clock. Headroom proportional to the clock, or always the quota's maximum, would protect long takeovers better but bill a forgotten job longer. If the sandbox reaches its timeout while the job is steered, the abandoned-sandbox backstop delivers the job and the verdict says the sandbox ended (DL-048, DL-045). With the default limits (4 hour jobs, 8 hour sandboxes) every allowed duration fits with the full hour of steering room. The session view reports the derived timeout, so the lifetime bar matches the job.

The job's idle timeout is set to that same derived timeout, and the request's and config's idle timeout apply to interactive sessions only (`TestAJobsSandboxIdlesOutOnlyAtItsTimeout`). Modal resets a sandbox's idle clock only on inbound requests. A job's agent makes outbound calls only, the recorder's held stream does not count, and spov never touches a sandbox on a timer (DL-018), so a job on the interactive 30-minute idle default would die mid-work. Touching the sandbox from the server to keep it alive was rejected: it would trade a correctness bug for a billing bug on every job.

### At the bound

The supervisor checks the clock after each pass, for `running` and `blocked` jobs only, from the row and the time. It reads nothing from the sandbox, and it runs even when reading events fails. The row is read locked (`JobStore.GetForUpdate`) so a resume that starts a stretch, or a steer that banks one, cannot land between the decision and the write. A stop the pass has just read wins over the bound.

When the clock is over, in this order:

1. The job moves to `delivering` with a note naming the bound and the time used, and the `spov.job.bounded` marker (`MarkerBounded`) is written in the same transaction.
2. Delivery, which runs only once the row says `delivering`, finds the marker and calls `EndRun` (`Service.EndJobRun`), best-effort: a sandbox that cannot be reached is collected as it is. That stops the loop for good with the plugin's own `bound` command, announced as `wall-clock` so the transcript does not attribute it to a person, then interrupts the turn with `resume=false` so nothing queued starts. No interrupt marker is written, and the job has already left `running`, so nothing can read the interrupt as a person steering. A redelivery after a crash does the same.
3. Delivery then runs as for any other stop: evidence and deliverables are collected while the sandbox is alive, the gates judge, and the sandbox is terminated (DL-050, DL-051).

The interrupt must come after the move to `delivering` (`TestARunningJobIsDeliveredAtItsWallClock`). The verdict opens with the bound note. A report job keeps whatever report exists and gate 2 judges it as usual, so the bound shows in the verdict rather than as a new state.

A blocked job at its bound is delivered the same way, through the `blocked -> delivering` edge in `jobTransitions`. The unanswered permission still vetoes at gate 3, so the outcome is a failure, now with a verdict. A steered job is not counted. It is bounded only by its sandbox timeout and is judged when the sandbox ends, which is the cost of not cutting off a person. An interrupted job is steered too (DL-053), so one that no one resumes waits for its sandbox timeout, at most `SteerHeadroom` plus the margin past its clock. A job that is steered and then abandoned bills until then, and Cancel job ends it at once.

### What is deliberately not done

- **Spend caps** per requester or per deployment. With a concurrency limit of 2 and this clock, one person's spend is bounded. Caps become necessary when something other than a person launches jobs, and are to be decided before any trigger ships. Cost figures are estimates of requested resources times wall-clock time (DL-009), so a per-job cost cap would be the clock times a constant. `deploy/README.md` says there is no spend cap beyond the clock and the concurrency limit, and that model tokens are paid through the provider keys the job runs with, which spov does not meter. The clock bounds model spend only indirectly.
- **Egress expiry.** The default egress stays as it is (DL-031); it is to be revisited before a trigger, or a principal who is not the operator, launches jobs.
- **Credential narrowing** per job kind (a `report` job getting no push token).
- **No-progress detection and a wrap-up turn** are not adopted. The clock bounds what a stalled loop costs.
- **Parking at the bound** and **failing outright at the bound**, for the reasons in the Context.

## Rationale

- Ending the run and judging it reuses the path any other stop takes, so a bound behaves like the loop giving out and the gates stay the only thing that decides success.
- One allowance keeps `max_duration` a statement of how much unattended time the job may use. With a fresh allowance on every resume, the duration someone set stopped meaning anything after the first takeover.
- A clock instead of a cost cap: a job's size is fixed when it is created, so a per-job cap is a second number to configure that bounds nothing the clock does not.
- The idle timeout protects against a sandbox nobody is using, which is the right signal for an interactive session whose client makes requests. For a job it is the wrong one: the job is in use for as long as it runs, and nothing inbound shows it. The bounds a job has are about the work, not about traffic.

## Measurements

- A report job whose agent fetched a repository every 4 minutes for an hour had its sandbox terminated by Modal exactly 30 minutes after the last inbound request (the recorder reattaching after a restart) while the agent was still working. The job failed because the out directory could not be read from a stopped sandbox. This is the case the idle timeout rule exists for.
- Constants: `DefaultWallClock` 1 h; `MinWallClock` 1 min; `WallClockMargin` launch budget (5 min) plus 5 min; `SteerHeadroom` 1 h; `sandbox.MaxTimeout` 12 h; default quotas 4 h job duration and 8 h sandbox.
- Against Postgres 18: the store contract including the wall-clock case, the blocked delivery edge and a `max_job_duration` override; `TestIntegrationJobLifecycleEndsAtItsWallClock` runs a job through the real supervisor to its clock, delivery and verdict; migration 00018 rolls down and up. Re-run: `go test -tags integration ./internal/server/ -run JobLifecycle` with `SPOV_TEST_DATABASE_URL` set.

## Consequences

- No number of continuations ends a job: it keeps going until its clock runs out.
- A job runs to its wall clock however quiet it is from outside.
- A user who wants more time after steering creates a new job. Extending a clock could be an explicit act later.
- `JobRequest` carries `max_duration` and `max_duration_preferred`; `MeView` and the profile update carry `default_job_duration`; the quota carries `max_job_duration`; `docs/configuration.md` documents `sandbox.job_max_duration`. `spov jobs create` and the form have no idle-timeout control.
- `blocked -> delivering` is an edge of the state machine, so the abandoned-sandbox backstop judges an abandoned blocked job too, and gate 3's veto still fails it (`TestTheAbandonedRoutingIsAnEdgeTheMachineHas`).
- The wall-clock stop is announced as itself (`TestTheWallClockStopIsAnnouncedAsItself`, `TestTheLoopCommandsAreOnesThePluginHandles`), and a conversation ID is escaped in the loop command and the model call (`TestEveryConversationPathIsEscaped`).

## Known gaps

- The launch log warns against the sandbox timeout, which nobody reads for a job; nothing at launch names the job's clock.
- Model token spend is invisible to spov, so there is no spend bound beyond time and concurrency.
